KYC and AML: Identity Verification and Money Laundering Prevention

KYC – KNOW YOUR CUSTOMER

WHAT IS KYC?
KYC (Know Your Customer) is the mandatory process by which a bank or financial institution verifies the identity of its customers before allowing them to open an account or conduct significant transactions. KYC is a legal requirement, not optional.

The purpose of KYC is to:
- Prevent fraud and identity theft.
- Prevent financial institutions from being used for money laundering or terrorist financing.
- Ensure that the bank knows who it is dealing with.

WHAT INFORMATION IS COLLECTED FOR KYC?
For individuals:
- Full legal name.
- Date of birth.
- Address (current and permanent).
- Nationality.
- Occupation and source of income.
- Government-issued photo ID (in India: Aadhaar card, PAN card, passport, voter ID, driver's licence).
- Photograph.

For businesses (Business KYC / KYCB):
- Certificate of incorporation.
- PAN of the company.
- Memorandum and Articles of Association.
- List of beneficial owners (anyone owning more than 25% of the company).
- Details of directors and authorised signatories.
- Proof of registered address.

TYPES OF KYC IN INDIA
1. In-Person Verification (IPV): customer visits a bank branch with original documents.
2. Video KYC (V-CIP): customer is verified via a video call by a bank representative.
3. Aadhaar-based eKYC: uses Aadhaar biometrics or OTP to verify identity against the UIDAI database digitally. The fastest and most common digital KYC method.
4. Offline Aadhaar KYC: customer shares a digitally signed XML file downloaded from UIDAI.
5. DigiLocker-based KYC: customer shares documents stored in their DigiLocker.

CIP – CUSTOMER IDENTIFICATION PROGRAM
A CIP is a formal written programme a bank maintains describing how it will identify and verify customers. Required by regulators.

RISK-BASED KYC
Not all customers are the same risk. Banks apply:
- Simplified Due Diligence (SDD): for low-risk customers with small accounts.
- Standard Due Diligence: for most customers.
- Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD): for high-risk customers such as PEPs (Politically Exposed Persons), customers from high-risk countries, and customers with unusually complex transaction patterns.

KYC REFRESH
KYC information must be updated periodically:
- High-risk customers: every two years.
- Medium-risk: every eight years.
- Low-risk: every ten years.
Banks may ask existing customers to submit fresh KYC documents even if the account is old.

WHAT HAPPENS IF KYC IS INCOMPLETE?
- Accounts may be frozen or restricted.
- Transactions above certain limits may be blocked.
- The RBI requires banks to close non-KYC-compliant accounts in some circumstances.

AML – ANTI-MONEY LAUNDERING

THE THREE STAGES OF MONEY LAUNDERING
1. Placement: introducing illegal cash into the financial system (e.g., depositing it in small amounts across multiple accounts).
2. Layering: moving the money through multiple transactions to obscure the trail (e.g., transfers between accounts, converting to crypto, international wires).
3. Integration: the money re-enters the economy appearing legitimate (e.g., purchasing property, investing in a business).

AML CONTROLS IN PAYMENT SYSTEMS
- Transaction monitoring: automated systems analyse each transaction and transaction history for red flags.
- Threshold reporting: transactions above regulatory thresholds are reported automatically (e.g., cash transactions above ₹10 lakh in India).
- Watchlist screening: names are checked against AML, sanctions, and PEP databases.
- Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) / Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs): filed by the bank when a transaction or customer behaviour is deemed suspicious. The customer is NOT told that a SAR/STR has been filed (called the "tipping off" prohibition).
- Record retention: banks must keep KYC and transaction records for a minimum number of years (5 years in India under PMLA).

COMMON RED FLAGS FOR AML
- Structuring: making multiple transactions just below reporting thresholds.
- Round-tripping: money going out and coming back to the same entity via a different route.
- Rapid movement: funds transferred quickly through multiple accounts without an obvious business reason.
- Cash-intensive businesses reporting unusually high revenues.
- Geographic red flags: transactions involving high-risk jurisdictions.
- Mismatched purpose: stated purpose of payment does not match transaction pattern.

FATF AND GLOBAL AML STANDARDS
The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) is an intergovernmental body that sets the global standards for AML and CFT. Countries are evaluated against FATF standards; countries on the "grey list" or "black list" face increased scrutiny on transactions involving their institutions.

INDIA-SPECIFIC AML FRAMEWORK
- PMLA (Prevention of Money Laundering Act) 2002: the primary AML legislation.
- FIU-IND (Financial Intelligence Unit – India): the central agency receiving, analysing, and disseminating STRs and CTRs.
- Regulated entities (banks, NBFCs, payment aggregators) must register with FIU-IND and file reports.
- The RBI issues Master Directions on KYC, which are updated periodically.
